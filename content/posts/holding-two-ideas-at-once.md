I am currently reading *Let Only Red Flowers Bloom*, a banned book in China and apparently now Hong Kong. The book depicts the government largely in a negative light—so it wasn't surprising that it was banned—and shattered many of my prior assumptions about the CCP. As many reviewers have noted, it is one thing to look at abstract data and cold statistics, but encountering raw, heartbreaking personal stories taps into something far more primal and intimate. This is where the book becomes a much more effective medium compared to other sources (that I have read, for what it's worth). And yet, the general sentiment on China on the corner of the internet I frequent seems to be more positive now than it has ever been. This morning, for example, I watched a video on China's lead in producing renewable energy and how it powers most of the world's electric vehicles, solar panels, and wind turbines. Across Reddit, I also see plenty of disillusioned Americans, exhausted by domestic politics, praising Xi Jinping’s sweeping anti-corruption drives[^1]. 

When I tried to collect these thoughts together, there seems to be some internal friction. I realised that the narratives pulled in opposed directions, one heavily critical, the other admiring. I grew frustrated with this internal resistance, because in reality both of these thoughts can be true at the same time, and refusing to let them coexist felt completely dumb and illogical. It is very easy to fall into—and relapse into—the fallacy of characterising things into a single binary: good or evil.

Most reasonable adults would readily acknowledge the truth that nothing is purely good nor purely evil. We do not study the history of things to determine whether they are purely good or evil, which, as established, simply does not exist, but rather to figure out which of their characteristics dominate: are they more evil or more good? While this revised definition is more nuanced, even this perspective is a bit too black-and-white when deliberated independently. Good and evil go beyond its simple definition; an entity can be mostly good in one aspect but be cruel and evil in another. True inquiry is about mapping where those achievements and atrocities lie, understanding the forces that drive them, and measuring the real human weight of both.

The trap is how easily we slide back into moral absolutism. It has significantly less cognitive load than the mechanism discussed earlier; no matter how many times I have learnt that this simplistic system is not an accurate representation of reality, I keep falling back into it nevertheless. Holding the full picture in working memory—tracking how a system causes harm in domain $X$ through mechanisms $A$ and $B$, while simultaneously delivering progress in domain $Y$ through mechanisms $C$ and $D$—demands continuous, active effort that our brains naturally resist. When a situation actually demands nuanced judgment, my brain’s default autopilot overrides what I consciously know, flattening these complex realities back into binary caricatures. And thus this mental shortcut often leads to wrong deductions and inferences, which in turn lead to wrong conclusions.

I am unsure how to break out of this vicious cycle, or if a clean way out even exists.

[^1]: Ironically in my book, these campaigns are usually a corruption act in itself. A notable example as written in the book, the [掃黑除惡](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hk/扫黑除恶专项斗争) campaign, had a lot of prosecutions solely on the basis of bribery, retribution, and power consolidation. This is not to say that it is _purely_ corrupt, but a lot of it certainly is.
